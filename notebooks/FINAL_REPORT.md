Paired difference (BS − Heston) across seeds: mean **-$1.76**, std $55.05,
standard error $8.70, t-stat **-0.20** — indistinguishable from zero given
the seed-to-seed noise (a mean gap of $1.76 against a per-seed P&L standard
deviation of over $100 is not a real edge either direction).

**This revises the earlier 10-seed read.** The orchestrator's preliminary
10-seed sweep (seeds 1–10) showed BS winning 7/10 with a mean gap of ~$5
(BS $71.8 vs Heston $66.9) — reproduced exactly here as a subset (seed 2:
Heston $108.9 vs BS $32.1; seed 6: BS $433.3 vs Heston $428.9; etc., all
match). At 40 seeds the win rate is exactly 50/50 and the mean gap shrinks
to -$1.76 (Heston now *nominally* ahead of BS on the mean, but well within
noise) — the 10-seed BS "edge" doesn't survive a larger sample. **Verdict:
this is a genuine wash on P&L and Sharpe in this setup**, not a slight edge
for either model.

One asymmetry does persist at 40 seeds and is worth flagging: **Heston's
drawdowns run somewhat deeper on average** (mean -$59.3 vs -$48.2, median
-$40.1 vs -$33.9). This is consistent with the delta mechanism in §4 below —
Heston's higher deltas mean bigger directional bets, which cost more when a
seed's realized path moves against the position before mean-reverting.

## 3. Regime case studies

Two seeds were selected automatically as the largest-gap outcomes in the
40-seed sweep (max BS-minus-Heston and max Heston-minus-BS), then explained
against their realized `variance_path`/`spot_path`.

### Case A — `market_seed=15`: Heston wins decisively ($156.8 vs -$1.6)

Figure: `data/case_study_seed15_heston_wins.png`

- Spot rallies steadily from 100 to ~113.5 (+13.5%), with a shallow max
  drawdown of only -3.4% along the way.
- Realized variance stays **low and falling** throughout: mean 0.029 vs the
  long-run `theta=0.045`, terminal variance 0.0062 — well below the
  stationary level, consistent with the calibrated `rho≈-0.66`: spot
  climbing while vol subsides is exactly the negative spot-vol correlation
  Heston's calibration captures and flat BS cannot.
- Heston's calibrated deltas are structurally higher than BS's flat-vol
  deltas on the same book state (per the orchestrator's earlier probe: e.g.
  0.782 vs 0.724, 0.679 vs 0.580, 0.543 vs 0.460 across contracts). In a
  quietly-rising, de-volatilizing market, that larger directional exposure
  is a pure tailwind — Heston ends up effectively "more long" through the
  rally with none of the adverse-vol-spike downside that would normally
  accompany extra delta, because vol was falling, not rising, this time.
  BS, with smaller deltas and a book calibrated to a static vol level that
  doesn't reflect the actual (falling) vol regime, captures much less of the
  move and ends effectively flat.

### Case B — `market_seed=25`: BS wins by the largest margin ($471.4 vs $306.99)

Figure: `data/case_study_seed25_bs_wins.png`

- Spot falls from 100 to ~93.75, with a **sharp intra-path drawdown of
  -17.6%** before partially recovering.
- Realized variance rises well **above** the long-run level: mean 0.0595,
  terminal 0.0836 vs `theta=0.045` — a genuine vol-of-vol/variance spike
  during the sell-off, the regime Heston's calibration is supposed to
  price correctly (rising vol as spot falls).
- Both models make money on this path (spread capture in a volatile,
  mean-reverting-ish market is lucrative for either quoter), but BS
  captures ~54% more. Tellingly, **Heston's max drawdown here is much
  smaller than BS's** (-$18.3 vs -$70.0) — Heston's vega-aware skew pulled
  the book toward safety more aggressively during the variance spike,
  which worked as risk management (a much shallower drawdown) but also
  left P&L on the table relative to BS, which rode the volatile
  spread-capture opportunity more aggressively and happened to come out
  ahead by period's end despite the rockier ride.

**Reading across both cases:** the mechanism is the same in both — Heston's
skew-aware deltas and vega-aware inventory management make it behave
differently from BS, not uniformly better. When the realized path matches
the qualitative shape Heston is calibrated to expect (spot up / vol down,
Case A), the extra delta exposure pays off cleanly. When realized vol spikes
in a way that triggers Heston's more defensive vega-driven skewing (Case B),
it reduces risk (smaller drawdown) but can also cap upside relative to a
model that isn't reacting to vega risk the same way. Neither case is a bug;
both are the intended mechanism operating as designed — it's genuinely
path-dependent which side it favors, which is exactly what the 40-seed wash
in §2 reflects in aggregate.

## 4. The "why": delta/skew mechanism (in plain English)

This is **not** a bug and **not** a risk-aversion tuning artifact. The
orchestrator verified before this analysis started that `DEFAULT_RISK_
AVERSION` produces nearly identical spread widths for BS vs. Heston on the
same book state (e.g. 1.2317 vs 1.2345 for one contract) — so the shared
risk-aversion constant isn't structurally favoring either model's spread
capture.

The real driver is a genuine, structural difference in **deltas**. On
identical book states, Heston's calibrated deltas run consistently higher
than BS's flat-vol deltas across every contract in the basket (e.g. 0.782 vs
0.724, 0.679 vs 0.580, 0.543 vs 0.460). This is a direct, mechanical
consequence of the calibrated negative spot-vol correlation (`rho≈-0.66`):
when spot rises, Heston's model expects vol to fall, which (via the
correlation term in Heston's own risk-neutral dynamics) increases the
option's sensitivity to spot relative to what a flat, uncorrelated BS vol
would imply — and symmetrically the reverse on the downside. A flat BS vol
has no way to represent this cross-effect at all; it prices every option as
if spot and vol move independently.

Because `market_maker.quoting.generate_quotes` uses each pricer's own
Greeks both to size the reservation-price skew and to decide how
aggressively to lean the book against inventory, this delta difference
compounds over a 63-bar backtest: the two quoters accumulate meaningfully
different inventory paths in response to the same realized market and the
same arrival process, and that accumulated difference is what shows up in
final P&L/Sharpe/drawdown. This is precisely the mechanism the whole project
was built to test — and the 40-seed sweep shows it cuts both ways depending
on the realized path (§3), roughly canceling out on average in this specific
setup (basket, horizon, risk-aversion tuning, and calibration protocol).
**It would be a mistake to conclude either "Heston's extra realism doesn't
matter" or "Heston is structurally better/worse" from this — the honest
read is "the mechanism is real and visible, but its net P&L effect is
path-dependent and averages out to roughly zero over this particular
40-seed sample."**

## 5. Vol surface fit quality (Agent 2's results, referenced not recomputed)

From `vol_models/CALIBRATION_NOTES.md` and `data/vol_model_comparison.png`
(both pre-existing, produced by `python -m vol_models.comparison` against
`pricing.vol_surface.generate_vol_surface`'s default 13-strike x 6-maturity
grid):

| Model | IV RMSE (vol points) |
|---|---|
| Black-Scholes (flat, single constant) | 0.0698 |
| Heston (calibrated) | 0.0147 |
| SABR (calibrated, global single smile) | 0.0339 |

Heston fits ~4.8x better than flat BS, ~2.3x better than global SABR. BS's
error is worst at short-dated, far-OTM-put strikes (single worst point:
K=70, T=1M, error 0.235 IV) — exactly where the synthetic surface's negative
skew and positive smile convexity are steepest and a single flat number has
no mechanism to track either. BS is closest to correct near-the-money at
longer maturities, where the true surface's skew/smile has decayed toward
its flatter long-run asymptote — which is mechanically why the backtest's
flat-vol quoter (calibrated to the true ATM 180-day vol, per
`backtest.quoter_calibration.compute_flat_vol_for_bs_quoter`) is not
obviously mispriced for the 4-to-8-month-maturity basket actually traded
here: the basket sits close to the region where BS's structural error is
smallest, which likely also contributes to why BS is competitive in the
backtest despite its much worse full-surface RMSE.

## 6. Honest limitations

These apply to the project as a whole, not just this analysis phase:

1. **Synthetic vol surface is not arbitrage-checked.** `pricing.vol_surface
   .generate_vol_surface`'s quadratic-in-log-moneyness parameterization is a
   hand-tuned qualitative stand-in for an equity-index smile; no explicit
   calendar-spread or butterfly no-arbitrage constraints are enforced (Agent
   1's own flagged limitation). Every downstream fit (Heston, SABR, the
   backtest's "true market") ultimately targets or derives from this
   surface's shape, so any latent arbitrage violation in the surface
   propagates everywhere else, silently.
2. **The Heston quoter never re-calibrates.** `backtest.quoter_calibration
   .calibrate_heston_quoter_params` runs once at t=0 against a noisy
   snapshot of the true market and is held fixed for the entire 63-bar
   backtest — no online re-estimation of `v0` (which is the fastest-moving
   state variable) or any other parameter as the market evolves (Agent 4's
   flagged limitation). A real desk would re-mark or filter at least `v0`
   daily; not doing so likely understates Heston's real-world edge in
   volatile regimes (Case B in §3 is a plausible example — a live-updating
   `v0` might have let the Heston quoter react to the variance spike rather
   than quote off a stale t=0 estimate).
3. **Additive, uncorrelated delta/vega risk combination in `market_maker
   .quoting`.** The reservation price and spread formulas sum independent
   delta-risk and vega-risk terms with no cross term, ignoring the very
   spot-vol correlation that makes Heston's deltas different from BS's in
   the first place (Agent 3's flagged limitation). A true portfolio-variance
   risk charge would include a `rho`-weighted cross term; both quoters in
   this backtest are risk-sized without one, even the Heston-informed one.
4. **Calls-only, 6-contract basket, no expiry handling.** `build_default_
   basket()` is 3 strikes x 2 maturities of calls, deliberately small so the
   report stays readable; puts, deep multi-strike/multi-tenor grids, and
   contract expiry/exercise mid-backtest are all out of scope. Results here
   should not be read as generalizing to a realistic, full-chain market-
   making book.
5. **Order flow is Poisson-with-exponential-intensity by construction**
   (`backtest.arrivals.poisson_fill_counts`, the classic Avellaneda-
   Stoikov/Guéant microstructure assumption) — not specific to this
   project's implementation, but a modeling assumption baked into AS-style
   quoting generally. Real order flow is bursty, correlated across time and
   across strikes, and reacts to realized (not just quoted) price moves in
   ways a memoryless Poisson process cannot capture; `base_intensity=80`
   and `order_arrival_kappa=1.5` are also uncalibrated placeholders (no real
   order-flow data was available to fit them against).
6. **Risk-aversion and arrival parameters were empirically grid-searched
   for stability, not derived.** `DEFAULT_RISK_AVERSION(delta=0.5,
   vega=0.02)` and `max_expected_arrivals_per_bar=3.0` were tuned to avoid
   a runaway inventory-accumulation spiral on this specific basket/intensity
   combination (Agent 4's flagged decision) — they are "reasonable and
   stable," not literature values, and the P&L comparison could shift
   somewhat under a different (still-stable) choice.
7. **P&L is marked to the true market's own Heston fair value for both
   quoters**, not to either quoter's own model or an observed market mid
   (Agent 4's flagged decision). This is the right choice for an apples-to-
   apples comparison (neither model can show phantom P&L from marking
   optimism) but means neither run's P&L series is literally "what a real
   desk's P&L screen would show."
